# UNSW-NB15 (2015)

## Paper Information

- **Title:** *UNSW-NB15: A Comprehensive Data Set for Network Intrusion Detection Systems*
- **Authors:** Nour Moustafa and Jill Slay
- **Affiliation:** School of Engineering and Information Technology, University of New South Wales at the Australian Defence Force Academy, Canberra, Australia
- **Year:** 2015

## One-Sentence Summary

UNSW-NB15 is a **network intrusion detection data set** that combines modern normal traffic with synthetically generated attacks and provides 49 attributes describing network flows, derived connection behavior, and labels.

## Research Problem

Evaluating a Network Intrusion Detection System (NIDS) requires a representative data set containing both normal and malicious network behavior. Older benchmarks such as KDDCUP99 and NSL-KDD have several limitations:

- They were generated many years ago and do not adequately **represent modern network traffic**.
- They contain only a limited range of attacks, especially modern low-footprint attacks.
- KDDCUP99 contains many **duplicate records**, which can bias a classifier toward frequently repeated samples.
- **Artifacts** such as distinctive Time to Live (TTL) values may allow a model to learn shortcuts instead of meaningful attack behavior.
- The training and test distributions may differ substantially, producing biased or unreliable evaluation results.

NSL-KDD reduces some of the duplication and class-imbalance problems in KDDCUP99, but it still inherits an outdated traffic environment and does not represent modern low-footprint attacks well.

## Background

A NIDS monitors network traffic to identify malicious activity. NIDSs are commonly divided into two categories:

1. **Misuse- or signature-based detection:** Matches observed activity against known attack signatures. It is effective for known attacks but may fail to detect previously unseen attacks.
2. **Anomaly-based detection:** Learns a profile of normal network behavior and treats significant deviations from that profile as suspicious. It may detect unknown attacks, but it can also produce false alarms.

The reliability of either approach depends heavily on the quality and representativeness of the data used for training and evaluation.

## Data-Generation Method

### Tools

The authors used the following tools to generate and process the data:

- **IXIA PerfectStorm:** Generated normal traffic and nine families of synthetic attacks. Its attack scenarios were informed by vulnerability information from the CVE database.
- **tcpdump:** Captured network packets and divided the output into PCAP files.
- **Argus:** Extracted flow-level attributes from the captured packets.
- **Bro-IDS:** Extracted connection, HTTP, and FTP information. Bro was later renamed Zeek.
- **Custom C# algorithms:** Generated twelve additional features from the extracted flow records.
- **SQL Server 2008:** Stored and matched the outputs produced by Argus and Bro-IDS.

### Testbed

The testbed contained three virtual servers connected to clients through two routers and a firewall. Servers 1 and 3 generated normal traffic, while Server 2 generated malicious activity. The firewall was configured to pass both normal and malicious traffic, and `tcpdump` ran on Router 1 to capture the packets.

```text
                IXIA PerfectStorm
           normal and attack traffic
                       |
              Three virtual servers
                       |
Clients -- Router 1 -- Firewall -- Router 2 -- Clients
               |
            tcpdump
               |
           PCAP files
               |
        Argus and Bro-IDS
               |
        Extracted features
               |
     Matching and C# algorithms
               |
       SQL Server database
               |
            CSV files
```

### Capture Periods

The authors conducted two traffic-capture sessions:

1. On January 22, 2015, they captured 16 hours of traffic at a rate of one attack per second, producing approximately 50 GB of data.
2. On February 17, 2015, they captured 15 hours of traffic at a rate of ten attacks per second, producing another 50 GB.

In total, the data set contains approximately 100 GB of captured traffic from 31 hours of simulation. Using two attack rates allowed the data set to include attacks with different intensity levels.

## Processing and Labeling Pipeline

```text
Raw network packets
        |
     tcpdump
        |
     PCAP files
        |
  Argus + Bro-IDS
        |
Initial flow features
        |
Flow matching and custom algorithms
        |
Additional connection features
        |
Ground-truth matching
        |
Database and CSV files
```

Each record represents a network flow and contains attributes with nominal, integer, floating-point, timestamp, or binary data types. The Argus and Bro-IDS outputs were matched using flow identifiers such as source and destination IP addresses, source and destination ports, and protocol.

The authors labeled the records by matching the processed flows against a ground-truth table generated from the IXIA attack report. The data set provides two label fields:

- `attack_cat`: the attack category, or the normal category;
- `label`: `0` for normal traffic and `1` for attack traffic.

## Feature Structure

The paper describes 49 attributes in total:

| Attribute group | Attribute numbers | Count | Description |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Flow features | 1-5 | 5 | Source and destination addresses, ports, and protocol |
| Basic features | 6-18 | 13 | State, duration, bytes, packets, TTL, loss, service, and load |
| Content features | 19-26 | 8 | TCP windows and sequence numbers, mean packet sizes, and HTTP content information |
| Time features | 27-35 | 9 | Jitter, timestamps, inter-packet times, and TCP round-trip timing |
| Additional features | 36-47 | 12 | General-purpose and recent-connection statistics generated by custom algorithms |
| Label fields | 48-49 | 2 | `attack_cat` and binary `label` |

The first 47 attributes describe the traffic and its derived connection behavior. The final two fields are labels rather than model input features. In particular, `attack_cat` must not be used as an input when predicting the binary `label`, because doing so would cause target leakage.

## Record Distribution

The complete data set contains 2,540,044 records: 2,218,761 normal records and 321,283 attack records. The attacks belong to nine families:

| Category | Records |
| --- | ---: |
| Fuzzers | 24,246 |
| Analysis | 2,677 |
| Backdoors | 2,329 |
| DoS | 16,353 |
| Exploits | 44,525 |
| Generic | 215,481 |
| Reconnaissance | 13,987 |
| Shellcode | 1,511 |
| Worms | 174 |

The class distribution is highly imbalanced. Normal traffic accounts for approximately 87.35% of all records, and Generic attacks account for about 67% of all attack records. Consequently, accuracy alone is not sufficient for evaluating a model trained on this data set.

## Output Files

The original release described in the paper contains four CSV files:

- `UNSW-NB15_1.csv`: 700,000 records
- `UNSW-NB15_2.csv`: 700,000 records
- `UNSW-NB15_3.csv`: 700,000 records
- `UNSW-NB15_4.csv`: 440,044 records

The records in these files are ordered by their final timestamp. The release also includes a ground-truth file and an event-list file containing attack categories and subcategories.

The commonly used predefined training and test files have a different schema from the original 49-attribute CSV files. For example, `UNSW_NB15_training-set.csv` contains 45 columns, including `id`, `attack_cat`, and `label`. These versions should be distinguished when describing preprocessing or model inputs.

## Main Contributions

The paper makes the following contributions:

1. It introduces a **more modern** NIDS benchmark than KDDCUP99 and NSL-KDD.
2. It combines **normal traffic with nine families of synthetically generated attacks**.
3. It provides packet-, flow-, content-, time-, and connection-based attributes.
4. It provides both binary and multi-class labels.
5. It releases processed CSV files and describes the pipeline used to derive them from approximately 100 GB of packet captures.

## Limitations and Threats to Validity

- The traffic was collected in **a controlled testbed**, so it cannot fully represent the diversity of a production network.
- The attacks were generated synthetically and may contain **artifacts specific** to IXIA PerfectStorm or the testbed configuration.
- The two capture sessions used different attack rates. A model may learn capture-session characteristics rather than general attack behavior.
- Some features, such as TTL, service, state, or recent-connection counts, may act as shortcuts that are specific to this environment.
- The attack categories are severely imbalanced, with very few examples of categories such as Worms and Shellcode.
- The paper compares the design of UNSW-NB15 with earlier data sets, but it does not establish real-world representativeness through extensive cross-network evaluation.

## Relevance to Distribution-Shift Research

UNSW-NB15 is useful for studying whether strong performance under random train-test splitting reflects genuine generalization:

- The original records are time-ordered, so a random split can place temporally adjacent and highly related flows in both the training and test sets.
- Several `ct_*` attributes summarize recent connections, which introduces dependencies between neighboring records.
- Random splitting can **mix the two capture sessions** even though they used different dates and attack rates.
- Models may rely on environment-specific shortcuts instead of transferable indicators of malicious behavior.

A more informative evaluation should compare random splitting with temporal splitting, capture-session splitting, and attack-category holdout experiments. Suitable metrics include per-class recall, macro-averaged F1 score, false-positive rate, and the confusion matrix rather than accuracy alone.
